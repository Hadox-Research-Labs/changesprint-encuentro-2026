Topic 01 / 27

Responsible AI in public services

Welcome. I am Edgar Valdés. In this first ChangeSprint workshop we will work on a central question: how can we design AI-supported decisions that can be explained, supervised and corrected? In public services, speed and technical sophistication are not enough. Decisions affect rights, benefits, opportunities and trust between institutions and the public. Responsible AI is therefore an institutional capability: preventing harm, detecting errors, explaining how a result was reached and correcting failures. The purpose is to make innovation sustainable, capable of learning and useful to the public without transferring unnecessary risks to people.

Topic 02 / 27

Two similar applicants, two different outcomes

Imagine two applicants with very similar profiles. One is selected and the other is not. If AI participated in the assessment, producing an outcome is only the beginning. What must exist for both people to trust the process, even when one disagrees? We need understandable criteria, relevant data, a record of each assessment and a person with authority to review the result. People also need a real route to request an explanation or report an error. This distinguishes automating a task from building a legitimate decision. Trust does not require identical outcomes; it requires differences that can be justified and corrected.

Topic 03 / 27

Name the decision before choosing AI

Before discussing models, prompts or agents, name the public decision you want to improve. Are you prioritising applications, checking requirements, describing information or supporting someone through a process? Each verb carries a different responsibility. Introduce AI once the problem, relevant evidence and accountable person are clear. Starting with the tool risks automating confusion. Starting with the decision allows you to compare alternatives and establish boundaries. Throughout this session, first define the decision, then ask whether AI adds value and what controls it requires.

Topic 04 / 27

AI learns, generates and can also act

This map separates concepts. Machine learning identifies patterns through supervised, unsupervised or reinforcement learning. Deep learning extends these capabilities, while generative AI produces new content such as text and images. Agentic AI is not a separate family of learning: it organises capabilities around an objective. An agent combines a model, instructions, memory and tools. It can plan steps, consult information and perform actions within defined limits. This matters in public services. A model produces an output; an agent can turn that output into a consequence. Greater ability to act requires clearer permissions, records, supervision and stop conditions.

Topic 05 / 27

Four verbs, four kinds of risk

We can understand AI uses through four verbs. Classify: organise cases or documents. Predict: estimate a future outcome, such as the possibility of dropping out. Generate: produce text, an explanation or a notification. Act: send, escalate or change something in a process. Each capability addresses a different task and introduces a different risk. Classification can exclude people because of incomplete data; prediction can confuse correlation with causation; generation can fabricate or oversimplify; action can proceed without authorisation. Specify the verb, data, criterion and institutional control, rather than simply saying that you will use AI.

Topic 06 / 27

Supporting the scholarship fund's process

AI can support several stages of the fund's process. It can explain requirements, identify missing documents, examine patterns and draft clear communications. It can even apply predefined criteria. However, supporting a task does not transfer institutional responsibility. Awarding a scholarship and approving exceptions require authority, traceability and review. If a tool proposes a classification, someone must be able to explain its criteria, check the data and correct the result. Technology can reduce operational workload. The institution must still be able to answer an applicant who asks why they were selected or excluded.

Topic 07 / 27

The agent coordinates; the institution decides

An agent can read a file, check requirements, prepare a notification, record an action and escalate an exception. Coordination can save time without making the agent a public authority. The institutional boundary must be written down: the agent does not award the scholarship. An accountable person validates the decision, can stop it and answers for it during review. Translate this boundary into technical permissions, records and mandatory human intervention points. Identify permitted actions, actions requiring authorisation and prohibited actions. A model produces an output; an agent can produce a consequence. Governance must develop alongside autonomy.

Topic 08 / 27

Responsible AI is not AI that never makes mistakes

No human or technological system can promise never to make mistakes. Responsible AI operates within an institution capable of responding. That capability has four movements: prevent harm before implementation; detect errors through monitoring and evidence; explain decisions to decision-makers and affected people; and correct outcomes, changing or stopping the system when necessary. This shifts attention from model quality alone to the preparedness of the surrounding institution. Accountability appears in procedures, roles, documentation and the possibility of remedy.

Topic 09 / 27

Four questions behind a legitimate public decision

Ethics, accountability, safety and security, and governance complement one another without meaning the same thing. Ethics asks whether we should do something and which rights or values are at stake. Accountability asks who answers, who reviews and who can provide a remedy. Safety and security ask how we prevent harm, manipulation, leaks and technical failures. Governance asks how rules, records, monitoring and suspension decisions control the system. A system can resist attacks yet remain unfair. Its intentions can be ethical while nobody is accountable. Trust requires all four dimensions to work together, with evidence that an outsider can verify.

Topic 10 / 27

Automation relocates discretion

Automation relocates rather than eliminates discretion. Someone chooses the input data, optimisation objective, acceptable outcome and points of human intervention. Consider four risk factors. Impact: does the decision affect rights, benefits or essential opportunities? Scale: how many people can be affected at once? Irreversibility: how difficult is it to repair the decision? Vulnerability: what is the person's position relative to the authority, and what real capacity do they have to defend their interests? Higher levels of these factors call for stronger controls. Technology can make administrative choices less visible. Our task is to make them explicit and auditable.

Topic 11 / 27

The institutional system is the real algorithm

The real algorithm extends beyond the model. It begins with data, passes through the provider or interface, reaches an institutional decision, is communicated and is eventually reviewed. Failures can occur at every point: incomplete data, incorrect recommendations, treating a recommendation as an order, concealing automation, or providing slow or nonexistent review. Responsibility cannot be delegated to the supplier. The institution using the system retains the obligation to justify its decision and respond to affected people. The cycle continues beyond delivery of a result until there is a real possibility of challenging, reviewing and correcting it.

Topic 12 / 27

Start with the problem

Before choosing a model, identify the specific friction to resolve and the evidence supporting the use of AI. Compare expected public value with risks of automation, exclusion, opacity and supplier dependence. Three questions discipline the proposal. What evidence shows that the problem needs AI? Is there a simpler, verifiable alternative? Can we run a controlled pilot with clear stop criteria? A sound decision may be to use AI, limit its role or avoid it. Innovation means finding an intervention that produces better evidence and can be controlled.

Topic 13 / 27

Fairness is measured in outcomes

Measure fairness in outcomes as well as intentions. An apparently neutral rule can produce very different results between groups because of historical data, proxy variables, differences in access or language, or circumstances the model does not understand. Use disaggregated tests before deployment and during operation. Examine variables that may act as proxies for origin, socioeconomic circumstances or other sensitive characteristics. Provide a channel to report unexpected patterns of rejection or exclusion. Ask what actually happens and what evidence demonstrates it. Fairness requires continuous verification.

Topic 14 / 27

Explain to enable challenge; supervise to enable correction

Meaningful human review requires three things. Capability: reviewers must understand the system, detect errors and recognise inadequate explanations. Time: they need operational conditions that allow them to review cases. Authority: they must be able to change outcomes, escalate cases and suspend processes. An explanation generated by a language model does not by itself demonstrate the real cause of a decision. It can be a convincing but incorrect reconstruction. Responsible explanation requires traceability, documented criteria and verifiable evidence.

Topic 15 / 27

Innovation requires layered defences

Removing a name and national identification number does not necessarily anonymise a narrative. Dates, family references, location and other details can identify someone again. Setting creativity to zero does not eliminate hallucinations or guarantee validity. User-supplied text must be treated as potentially risky input, especially when it can contain instructions aimed at the model. Combine data minimisation, access controls, validation, separation of instructions from content, records, testing, human review and monitoring. No single defence is sufficient. The institution remains responsible for the outcome even when an external platform processes the data.

Topic 16 / 27

International references for institutional design

Useful references already exist. Canada uses algorithmic impact assessments to adjust controls to risk. The United Kingdom promotes public transparency records showing where and how algorithms are used. New Zealand developed an algorithm charter covering transparency, data and community participation. Understand the institutional principle behind each mechanism rather than copying forms. Higher risk requires stronger controls. Automation affecting people should be visible. Affected communities should participate when sensitive categories and fairness criteria are defined. FONABE can adapt these ideas into its own proportionate design.

Topic 17 / 27

From principles to verifiable controls

International practice has developed through three complementary stages. First came principles and rights: dignity, fairness, transparency and accountability. Next came risk-based obligations: assessing impacts, identifying affected people and establishing safeguards. The third stage requires operational evidence: recording, explaining, monitoring and correcting. Agentic systems add tools and multi-step actions. Assessing response quality alone is insufficient. Set explicit boundaries, name an accountable person, supervise actions and preserve traceability. Document, explain, allow challenges and correct failures before they become institutional harm.

Topic 18 / 27

ILIA measures ecosystems

The Latin American Artificial Intelligence Index, ILIA, examines the ecosystem that enables sustainable AI use. Its first dimension covers enabling factors: infrastructure, data and human talent. Its second covers research, development and adoption: the production and use of knowledge. Its third covers governance: institutional vision, international engagement and regulation. Guatemala has made progress while continuing to face capacity gaps. For FONABE, choosing a good model cannot compensate for weak data, limited supervision or missing rules. Maturity is demonstrated by the institutional ability to govern use, rather than access to a particular model.

Topic 19 / 27

Guatemala 2026: a window of opportunity

The original Guatemala workshop places the discussion in an evolving institutional context: UNESCO's readiness assessment, work towards a national AI strategy and debate about data protection. The absence of a specific law does not remove institutional responsibility. It creates an opportunity to design safeguards that may become useful references: document decisions, limit high-risk uses, protect data, provide review and evaluate pilots before scaling. The objective is to build defensible capabilities today that can support future standards. These contextual observations belong to the original workshop; they are not a legal update for another jurisdiction.

Topic 20 / 27

Turn preparation into design decisions

Each participant brings an answer from the preparatory assignment. Integrate those answers into one team hypothesis. Name the concrete problem, identify where AI might help and what harm it could cause, define a boundary that must not be automated, add a safeguard allowing supervision, appeal or stopping, and write the question still requiring investigation. This is a testable, explainable and correctable hypothesis, not a finished solution. Take four minutes to compare answers and agree on a common formulation.

Topic 21 / 27

Three challenges, three design opportunities

Team 1 examines discretion. Applicants perceive arbitrariness when they cannot understand their score. What information and evidence would make shortlisting defensible? Team 2 examines the application file. AI may shorten processing times while creating barriers through automatic rejection, invalid formats or incomplete information. What must a person review before discarding a document? Team 3 examines governance. A dynamic system requires clear rules. What controls, metrics and contractual arrangements must exist before, during and after a call for applications? All three teams must turn abstract principles into concrete choices.

Topic 22 / 27

Return to the opening dilemma

Two similar people receive different outcomes. What must exist for both to trust the process, even if one does not receive a scholarship? “The computer decided” is insufficient. We need evidence of the criteria applied, a traceable case history, an understandable explanation and a route to review. This dilemma tests the legitimacy of every proposal. If a team cannot explain the difference to an affected person and show how it would correct an error, the solution is not yet responsible.

Topic 23 / 27

Team 1: protect the fund against arbitrariness

Make visible a specific point where a decision can change. Do not audit the entire fund. Choose one point where the same evidence could lead to different outcomes: applying a criterion, granting an exception or making an unrecorded change. Specify the criterion and how to check consistent application. Deliver a map of that discretionary point, a verifiable rule and the evidence needed to audit it. Preserve legitimate human judgement while distinguishing it from arbitrariness and identifying who is accountable.

Topic 24 / 27

Team 2: bridge shortlisting and actual receipt of support

Follow the case until the scholarship is actually delivered. Reconstruct what happens after shortlisting: requested documents, who supplies them, where time is lost and where someone may drop out. Identify one priority bottleneck and propose a support mechanism. If AI is involved, specify its task and what a person must verify before rejecting a document or closing a file. Deliver the case journey, the bottleneck and an intervention that reduces burden without creating another automatic barrier.

Topic 25 / 27

Team 3: governance and responsible AI use

Design the rules before the tool. Define the problem AI may support and the decision explicitly outside its scope. Assign three responsibilities: administration, supervision and accountability for outcomes. Define mandatory safeguards: minimum data, bias testing, records, explanation, human review and a clear suspension condition. Deliver a use rule naming owners, safeguards and stop criteria. The objective is a documented, supervisable and auditable process, rather than another model recommendation or access to confidential prompts.

Topic 26 / 27

Cross-review proposals

Before a pilot is approved, another team reviews five questions. Is the problem demonstrated? Are data sufficient and relevant? Does expected benefit outweigh risk? Is there review and a route to appeal? Will evidence remain for an audit? Review aims to find harm before it occurs. Recommend one of three outcomes: approve the pilot, correct specified conditions, or stop until sufficient evidence exists. Identify what is missing, who must address it and what test would justify reconsideration. Institutional authorisation remains a human responsibility.

Topic 27 / 27

Innovation improves the evidence

For the next forty minutes, each team prepares its strongest proposal for a ninety-second presentation. Select the idea that best combines value for the person, institutional feasibility and controllable risk. Success means solving the problem well and explaining how errors will be prevented, detected and corrected. State the decision to improve, what will remain outside automation and how accountability will work. This discipline forms the basis of the next workshop, where ideas become checklists and working prototypes.
