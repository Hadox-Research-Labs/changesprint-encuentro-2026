# Ethical and responsible AI in public policy

## 0. Ethical and responsible AI in public policy

Welcome, everyone. This masterclass proposes a journey through the public policy cycle using a guided example: a visual activation, generative feedback and a conversation about where artificial intelligence can intervene without displacing evidence, deliberation or human responsibility. The objective is not to automate public policy, but to learn to design verifiable and governable agentic support.

## 1. Imagine a more pleasant journey

We open with a voluntary invitation: imagine a more pleasant everyday journey using geometric shapes. We do not write names, addresses or identifiable routes. You can participate without submitting a drawing. The marks we see are hypothetical examples. AI can support every phase of public policy, but never replaces empirical evidence, legitimacy, deliberation or responsibility. The exercise's video will be creative feedback, not a diagnosis of Guatemala.

## 2. Established foundations

Our proposal starts from existing work, not a claim to have invented AI use in public policy. Valle-Cruz and colleagues study the dynamic public policy cycle. Medaglia analyses risks and opportunities, including emerging agentic systems. The OECD collects government applications and capabilities. These works help formulate questions and compare practices; they do not automatically validate our local case. Sources: Valle-Cruz et al. (2020), doi:10.1016/j.giq.2020.101509; Medaglia (2025), CBS Research Portal; OECD (2025), doi:10.1787/795de142-en.

## 3. The value and limits of a qualitative signal

A drawing is a voluntary expression. It can open a conversation and indicate a question worth investigating. It is not a representative sample, a measurement of preferences or a mandate to intervene. Separate what is observable from what is interpreted, and preserve doubts. If two circles allow several readings, we do not choose one because it is more visually convincing. Triangulation requires other sources and comparison with people's perspectives.

## 4. Roles of international frameworks

The organisations play different roles. The OECD compares government applications; UNESCO contributes ethical principles and rights; UNDP works on capabilities and institutional readiness; the World Bank documents emerging practices; NIST provides risk management and technical work on agents. None of these frameworks replaces deliberation or the local institution's responsibility. The illustration's symbols are graphical representations, not institutional endorsements. Full references are in fuentes.md.

## 5. AI technology families

Symbolic AI means explicit rules. Machine learning identifies patterns and enables classification or prediction. Deep learning is part of that field. Foundation models adapt to different tasks and modalities. An agentic system organises models, state, instructions and tools to execute tasks within limits. The circle diagram helps organise concepts, but is not an exhaustive classification and does not imply that every foundation model is an agent. Sources: Bommasani et al., arXiv:2108.07258; Yao et al., arXiv:2210.03629.

## 6. Context, fairness and practical judgement

This is our only philosophical pause. Floridi and Cowls let us ask who benefits, what harms are prevented and who can understand or question a decision. Nissenbaum requires respect for information flows appropriate to context: submitting a drawing for a class does not authorise every reuse. Vallor highlights virtues, care, humility and practical judgement. These perspectives guide concrete decisions about data, permissions and boundaries; they do not replace research.

## 7. Anatomy of an agent

The model interprets and proposes. Instructions bound the objective and criteria. Memory preserves state and references. Planning proposes steps and adjusts them to observations. Tools act on files or systems. Permissions must be enforced outside the model, through software. Evaluation reviews results, and human oversight can reject, stop or correct. An observable log records inputs, actions and outputs; it does not prove that a verbal explanation reflects all internal reasoning. Sources: ReAct, NIST AI RMF and NIST AI 600-1.

## 8. Verifiable agentic public policy

This is a proposed operating model pending empirical validation. Its value lies in requiring a bounded mandate, authorised inputs, restricted tools, reviewable artifacts and human responsibility. The illustrated seal is not scientific validation. Traceability allows changes to be reviewed, but does not by itself authenticate people or make a decision legitimate. An instruction can be versioned by an authority; it must not be silently modified by received content.

## 9. The Mirror: creative feedback

The player presents One Path, Three Perspectives, a four-minute, twenty-one-second pilot built from twenty fictional AI-generated examples. The montage moves through drawings, an audiovisual synthesis and a generative interpretation. No material comes from real participants or represents the gathering's results. Observe what the interpretation preserved, what it added and what still needs to be asked. A finished film is a product; it does not demonstrate public impact or representative support.

## 10. Problem construction and diagnosis

During problem construction, the agent proposes formulations and records doubts, producing a provisional sheet. Defining the problem and consulting affected people remain human tasks. In diagnosis, it can compare sources and flag contradictions, producing a triangulation matrix with explicit gaps. Validating quality, relevance and causation requires research. At each stage answer: what happens, what does AI do, what artifact does it leave and which decision remains human?

## 11. Objectives and alternatives

When defining objectives, AI can help distinguish outputs from effects and draft candidate indicators. The theory of change must preserve assumptions and open issues. Prioritising values and burdens requires deliberation. When formulating alternatives, AI can broaden options and document requirements, including the option of not using AI. The alternatives catalogue is not an approved budget or a prediction of effectiveness. The authority decides which options proceed to appraisal and under which criteria.

## 12. Appraisal and decision

Appraisal organises criteria, units, weights and sensitivity. It must not fill gaps with invented figures. The matrix documents what we know and what is missing. Weighting values and setting admissibility thresholds are explicit human decisions. At the decision stage, AI can synthesise reasons and disagreements; the minutes record the process. The legitimate authority decides and answers publicly. Consensus synthesised by a model is not equivalent to consent.

## 13. Implementation and monitoring

Implementation requires owners, permissions, records and suspension mechanisms. The agent can execute authorised tasks in bounded tools; it does not receive general power over systems. The log must preserve actions and versions. During monitoring, it tracks previously agreed indicators and prepares alerts. Alerts require interpretation: not every change is an improvement, and not every correlation explains a cause. The institution decides when to investigate, correct or stop and how to remedy effects.

## 14. Evaluation and iteration

Distinguish a finished video from a public effect such as walkability. Our exercise produces a communication artifact, not a causal evaluation. AI can organise reproducible analyses and competing explanations. The report must state the limits of data and methods. Judging public value and sufficiency of evidence remains a human responsibility. Iteration means reviewing objectives, data, rules or the programme's continuation itself, preserving versions and documented decisions.

## 15. Responsibility and deliberation

AI can support interpretation, documentation and coordination within verifiable limits. It does not by itself guarantee better decisions or complete traceability. A policy's meaning, the distribution of value and final responsibility depend on people and institutions. Return to the opening drawing: what could we see, what did we infer and what would we need to investigate before acting? Let us close with questions and a concrete decision each participant would like to be able to explain and review.
