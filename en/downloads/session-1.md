# Responsible AI in public services

## 1. Responsible AI in public services

Welcome, everyone. I am Edgar Valdés, and in this first ChangeSprint workshop we will work on a central question: how do we design decisions supported by artificial intelligence that can be explained, supervised and corrected? In the public sector, it is not enough for a tool to be fast or technically impressive. Decisions affect rights, benefits, opportunities and the relationship of trust between an institution and the public. That is why we will discuss responsible artificial intelligence as an institutional capability: preventing harm, detecting errors, explaining how a result was reached and correcting things when something fails. We are not trying to hold back innovation. We want it to be sustainable, to learn and to generate public value without transferring unnecessary risks to people.

## 2. Two similar applicants, two different outcomes

Imagine two applicants with very similar profiles. One person is selected and the other is not. If an artificial intelligence system participated in the assessment, the question is not merely whether it produced an outcome. The question is what would need to exist for both people to trust the process, even when one of them disagrees with the decision. We need understandable criteria, relevant data, a record of how each case was assessed and a person with authority to review the outcome. We also need a real way to request an explanation or point out an error. This example shows the difference between automating a task and building a legitimate decision. Trust does not demand identical outcomes; it demands that the difference can be justified and corrected.

## 3. Before using AI, name the decision

Before talking about models, prompts or agents, let us name the public decision we want to improve. Are we prioritising applications, checking requirements, describing information or supporting a person through the process? Each verb entails a different responsibility. Artificial intelligence should come in afterwards, once we know what the problem is, what evidence counts and who will answer for the outcome. If we start with the tool, we risk automating confusion. If we start with the decision, we can compare alternatives and establish limits. Throughout this session, let us keep a simple rule: first define the decision; then ask whether artificial intelligence adds value and under what controls.

## 4. AI learns, generates and can also act

This map helps us avoid mixing concepts. Machine learning identifies patterns through supervised, unsupervised or reinforcement learning. Deep learning extends that capability, and generative artificial intelligence produces new content, such as text or images. Agentic artificial intelligence is not another family of learning: it is a way of organising capabilities to achieve an objective. An agent combines a model, instructions, memory and tools. It can plan steps, consult information and execute actions within defined limits. That difference matters in public services. A model produces an output; an agent can turn that output into a consequence. Therefore, the greater its ability to act, the clearer its permissions, records, supervision and suspension condition must be.

## 5. AI is not a single thing: four verbs

We can understand artificial intelligence uses through four verbs. Classifying means organising cases or documents. Predicting means estimating a future outcome, such as the possibility of dropping out. Generating means producing a text, an explanation or a notification. Acting means sending, escalating or modifying something in the process. Each capability addresses a different task and introduces a different risk. Classification can exclude because of incomplete data; prediction can confuse correlation with causation; generation can fabricate or oversimplify; action can execute without authorisation. So the useful question is not simply whether we will use artificial intelligence. We must specify the verb, the data, the criterion and the institutional control.

## 6. AI can support the fund's process

At the fund, artificial intelligence can support different stages of the process. It can guide a person by explaining requirements, check for missing documents, review patterns or draft clear communications. It can even apply previously defined criteria. But there is a boundary we must not erase: supporting a task is not equivalent to transferring institutional responsibility. Awarding a scholarship, and any exception, require authority, traceability and the possibility of review. If a tool proposes a classification, someone must be able to explain the criteria, check the data and correct the outcome. Technology can reduce operational workload, but it cannot become the final answer to a person asking: why was I selected, or why was I left out?

## 7. Agentic AI coordinates; the institution decides

An agent can read an application file, check requirements, prepare a notification, record an action and escalate an exception. That coordination can save time, but it does not make the agent a public authority. The institutional boundary must be written down: it does not award the scholarship. An accountable person validates the decision, can stop it and answers during a review. This boundary is not decorative. It must translate into technical permissions, records and mandatory points of human intervention. If the system can act, we need to know which actions are permitted, which require authorisation and which are prohibited. A model produces an output; an agent can produce a consequence. Governance must therefore grow at the same pace as its autonomy.

## 8. Responsible AI is not AI that never makes mistakes

Responsible artificial intelligence is not artificial intelligence that never makes mistakes. No human or technological system can promise that. It is AI operating within an institution capable of responding. That capability has four movements. First, prevent harm before implementation. Second, detect errors through monitoring and evidence. Third, explain decisions to those who make them and those affected by them. Fourth, correct outcomes and modify or stop the system when necessary. This definition changes the focus. We stop asking only how good the model is and begin asking how prepared the surrounding institutional system is. Responsibility appears in procedures, roles, documentation and the possibility of remedy.

## 9. Four questions underpin a legitimate public decision

Ethics, accountability, safety and security, and governance complement each other, but they do not mean the same thing. Ethics asks whether we should do it and which rights or values are at stake. Accountability asks who answers, who reviews and who can provide a remedy. Safety and security ask how we prevent harm, manipulation, leaks or technical failures. Governance asks how the system is controlled through rules, records, monitoring and suspension decisions. A solution can be secure against attacks and still be unfair. It can be ethical in intention yet have nobody accountable. Trust appears when all four dimensions work together and an outsider can verify the evidence, rather than merely accepting an institutional promise.

## 10. Automation relocates discretion

Automation does not eliminate discretion; it relocates it. Someone decides what data go in, what objective is optimised, what outcome is considered acceptable and when a person may intervene. To assess risk, I suggest looking at four factors. Impact: does the decision affect rights, benefits or essential opportunities? Scale: how many people can be affected at the same time? Irreversibility: how difficult is it to repair the decision afterwards? Vulnerability: what is the person's position relative to the authority, and what real ability do they have to defend their interests? The greater these four factors, the less reasonable it is to rely on automation without controls. Technology does not remove the administrative decision; it often makes it less visible. Our job is to make it explicit and auditable.

## 11. The institutional system is the real algorithm

The real algorithm does not end at the model. It starts with data, passes through the supplier or interface, reaches an institutional decision, is communicated and is finally reviewed. A failure can arise at every point. Data may be incomplete. The model may produce an incorrect recommendation. The institution may treat it as an order. Communication may conceal that automation was involved. Review may be too slow or nonexistent. This is why responsibility cannot be delegated to the supplier. The institution using the system retains the obligation to justify its decision and respond to the affected person. Nor does the cycle end when a result is delivered; it ends when there is a real possibility of challenging, reviewing and correcting it.

## 12. Start with the problem, not the technology

The first condition is to start with the problem rather than the technology. Before asking which model we can use, ask what concrete friction we want to resolve and what evidence justifies bringing in artificial intelligence. Then compare the expected public value with the risks of automation, exclusion, opacity or dependence on a supplier. Three questions help discipline the idea. What evidence demonstrates that this problem needs artificial intelligence? Is there a simpler, verifiable alternative? Can we run a controlled pilot with clear stopping criteria? A good decision may be to use AI, limit its role or not use it. Innovation does not mean always choosing the most complex tool; it means finding an intervention that produces better evidence and can be controlled.

## 13. Fairness is measured in outcomes

The second condition is to measure fairness in outcomes, not only intentions. We can design an apparently neutral rule and still obtain very different results between groups. This can happen because of historical data, indirect variables, differences in access, language or circumstances the model does not understand. We therefore need disaggregated testing before and during operation. We must also examine variables that may act as proxies for origin, socioeconomic status or other sensitive characteristics. Finally, there must be a channel to report unexpected patterns of rejection or exclusion. The question is not whether the team had a fair intention. The question is what actually happens and what evidence we have to demonstrate it. Fairness must be verified continuously.

## 14. Explain to enable challenge; supervise to enable correction

Explanation enables challenge, and supervision enables correction. For human review to be real, it needs three pillars. The first is capability: reviewers must understand the system, detect an error and recognise when an explanation is insufficient. The second is time: they need operational conditions that allow them to review cases, not just a responsibility written in a manual. The third is authority: they must be able to change the outcome, escalate the case or suspend the process. There is an important technical qualification: a justification generated by a language model does not by itself demonstrate the true cause of the decision. It can be a convincing but incorrect reconstruction. Responsible explanation requires traceability, documented criteria and verifiable evidence.

## 15. Innovation requires layered defences

Responsible innovation needs layered defences. In privacy, removing the name and the DPI, Guatemala's personal identification number, does not necessarily make a narrative anonymous. Dates, family references, location and other details may identify someone again. For robustness, setting creativity to zero does not eliminate hallucinations or guarantee validity. For security, all text entered by a user must be treated as potentially risky input, particularly when it may contain instructions addressed to the model. No single defence is sufficient. Combine data minimisation, access controls, validation, separation of instructions and content, records, testing, human review and monitoring. Even if processing takes place on an external platform, the institution retains responsibility for the outcome.

## 16. Global references for institutional design

We do not need to invent every mechanism from scratch. Useful references exist. Canada uses algorithmic impact assessments to adjust controls to the level of risk. The United Kingdom promotes public transparency records to make visible where and how algorithms are used. New Zealand developed an algorithm charter incorporating commitments to transparency, data and community participation. The lesson is not to copy forms. It is to understand the institutional principle behind each mechanism. Higher risk calls for stronger controls. When automation affects people, its existence should be visible. And when sensitive categories or fairness criteria are defined, affected communities should participate. For FONABE, these references can become components of its own proportionate design.

## 17. Current practice already requires verifiable controls

International practice has advanced through three complementary stages. First came principles and rights: dignity, fairness, transparency and accountability. Then came obligations differentiated by risk: assess impacts, identify affected people and establish safeguards. The third stage requires operation with evidence: record, explain, monitor and correct. The most recent frontier is agentic systems, capable of using tools and executing several steps. There, evaluating the quality of a response is no longer sufficient. We must set explicit boundaries, designate an accountable person, supervise actions and preserve traceability. The common trend is clear: document, explain, allow challenges and correct before an isolated failure becomes institutional harm.

## 18. ILIA measures ecosystems, not isolated tools

The Latin American Artificial Intelligence Index does not assess only which model a country uses. It examines the ecosystem that enables sustainable use. The first dimension is enabling factors: infrastructure, data and human talent. The second is research, development and adoption: how much knowledge is produced and how it is used. The third is governance: institutional vision, international engagement and regulation. Guatemala has shown progress but still faces capacity gaps. For FONABE the lesson is direct: choosing a good model does not compensate for poor data, limited supervisory capacity or absent rules. Maturity is not demonstrated by access to Gemini; it is demonstrated by the institutional ability to govern its use.

## 19. Guatemala 2026: a window of opportunity

Guatemala is in a window of opportunity. The readiness assessment carried out with UNESCO, the development of a national artificial intelligence strategy and discussion of data protection show that the institutional framework is evolving. For FONABE, the absence of a specific law does not remove responsibility. On the contrary, it opens an opportunity to design safeguards now that could become a reference. The institution can document decisions, limit high-risk uses, protect data, create review mechanisms and evaluate pilots before scaling. The objective is not to wait for a perfect rule. It is to build defensible capabilities today that are compatible with the standard the country will need tomorrow.

## 20. Turn the assignment into design decisions

Now we will turn the preparatory assignment into design decisions. Each person arrives with an answer; the team must integrate those answers into a single hypothesis. First, name the concrete problem you want to improve. Then define where artificial intelligence might help and what harm it might cause. Set a boundary: what must not be automated. Add a safeguard that allows supervision, appeal or stopping. Finally, write the question you still need to investigate. We are not yet looking for a finished solution. This stage should produce a hypothesis that can be explained, tested and corrected. You have four minutes to compare answers and agree on a shared formulation.

## 21. Three challenges, three design opportunities

We will apply these principles to three design challenges. Team one works on discretion: a person perceives arbitrariness when they cannot understand why they received a particular score. Their question is what information and evidence would make shortlisting defensible. Team two works on the application file: artificial intelligence can reduce time but also create new barriers through automatic rejections, invalid formats or incomplete information. Their question is what a person must check before discarding a document. Team three works on governance: a dynamic system needs clear rules. Their question is what controls, metrics and contracts must exist before, during and after a call for applications. All three challenges share a goal: turn abstract principles into concrete decisions.

## 22. Return to the opening dilemma

Let us return to the opening dilemma. Two similar people receive different outcomes. After reviewing risks, controls and references, we can formulate the question better. What would need to exist for both people to trust the process, even if one does not receive the scholarship? The answer cannot simply be that the computer decided. We need evidence of the criteria applied, traceability of the case, an understandable explanation and a route to review the outcome. This dilemma serves as a legitimacy test for any proposal. If the team cannot explain the difference to the affected person and show how it would correct an error, it does not yet have a responsible solution.

## 23. Team 1: protect the fund against arbitrary discretion

Team one must make visible where a decision can change. Do not try to audit the entire fund. Choose one concrete point where the same evidence could produce different outcomes: application of a criterion, an exception or a change that left no record. Then define which criterion must be made explicit and how you would verify that it was applied consistently. The deliverable is a map of that discretionary point, accompanied by a verifiable rule and the evidence that would allow it to be audited. The goal is not to eliminate all human judgement; it is to distinguish legitimate discretion from arbitrariness and make clear who answers for the decision.

## 24. Team 2: bridge the gap between shortlisted applicants and beneficiaries

Team two must follow the case until the scholarship actually materialises. Reconstruct the journey after shortlisting: which documents are requested, who supplies them, where time is lost and when a person may drop out. Identify a priority bottleneck, then propose a support mechanism. If you use artificial intelligence, state precisely which task it supports and what information a person must check before rejecting a document or closing an application file. The deliverable is the case journey, a clearly identified bottleneck and an intervention that reduces burden without creating a new automatic barrier.

## 25. Team 3: AI governance and responsible use

Team three must design the rules before the tool. First define which problem artificial intelligence may support and which decision is explicitly outside the system. Then assign three responsibilities: who administers, who supervises and who answers for the outcome. Finally, define mandatory protections: minimum data, bias testing, records, explanation, human review and a clear suspension condition. The deliverable is a use rule with responsible people, safeguards and suspension criteria. The objective is not to recommend another model or obtain the confidential prompt. It is to make the complete process supervisable, documented and auditable.

## 26. Cross-review of proposals

Before a pilot is approved, a different team will review the proposal using five questions. Is the problem demonstrated? Are the data sufficient and relevant? Does the expected benefit outweigh the risk? Is there review and a route to appeal? Will evidence remain for an audit? The reviewing team is not trying to destroy the idea; it is trying to find harm before it occurs. Its recommendation must be one of three: approve the pilot, correct specific conditions or stop it until sufficient evidence is available. A useful review does not end with a yes or no. It identifies what is missing, who must address it and what test would allow the decision to be reconsidered.

## 27. Innovation means improving the evidence

We close with one idea: innovation means improving the experience while leaving evidence. Over the next forty minutes, each team will prepare its best proposal for a ninety-second presentation. The filter is simple: choose the idea that best combines value for the person, institutional feasibility and controllable risk. The proposal using the most artificial intelligence does not win. The one that solves the problem better and can explain how it will prevent, detect and correct errors wins. At the end, each team must be able to say what decision it wants to improve, what it will not automate and how it will be accountable. That discipline will underpin the next workshop, where ideas become checklists and working prototypes.
